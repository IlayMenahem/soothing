\section{Introduction}

A Gr\"obner basis is the standard tool for solving a system of multivariate polynomial equations $p_1 = \cdots = p_m = 0$: from it one can describe the solution set, eliminate variables, and decide ideal membership. Computing one, however, has doubly exponential worst-case complexity, and in practice becomes infeasible as the number of variables or the polynomial degrees grow.

For some problems we only need a Gr\"obner basis of a \emph{subvariety} of the variety $V(p_1, \ldots, p_m)$ --- a subset of the solutions that is cheaper to describe. We obtain such a subvariety by zeroing a chosen set of variables and selecting one irreducible factor of each polynomial, which replaces the original system with a smaller, often triangular one whose Gr\"obner basis is tractable. Every point of the subvariety is a genuine solution of the original system, so the output is sound; what we forgo is completeness, as solutions outside the subvariety are not recovered. This lets the method solve some systems that are out of reach for the standard algorithms.

The main drawback is that the subvariety may have much smaller dimension than the original variety. We show (Claim~2) that this loss comes only from the zeroed variables, not from factor selection, so the framework can target a subvariety of maximal dimension among those reachable by factor selection. The rest of the document formalizes the framework and its soundness (Section~2), and presents a CDCL-style algorithm that explores the minimal hitting sets of the system to decide satisfiability and related properties (Section~3).

\section{Framework}
The framework specifies methods for finding the Gr\"obner basis of a subvariety of the variety generated by a set of multivariate polynomials. The idea is to cut the system down before computing anything: zeroing variables removes them from every polynomial, and factoring lets us replace each polynomial $p'_j$ by a single irreducible factor, a weaker equation whose zero locus lies inside $V(p'_j)$. Both operations shrink the variety to a subvariety while leaving a smaller, more tractable system.

Given a set of multivariate polynomials $p_1, \ldots, p_m$, we perform the following preprocessing:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Select a subset of the variables to be zeroed, and substitute $0$ for those variables in the polynomials.
    \item (optional) Interreduce the polynomials with respect to a lexicographic order (using a min-fill heuristic variable ordering) to obtain new polynomials $p'_1, \ldots, p'_{m}$, and discard any zero polynomials.
    \item Find the factorization of each polynomial into
    \[
        \bigl\{\,(f,\;\{p'_j \mid f \text{ appears in } p'_j\})\;\bigm|\; f \text{ is an irreducible factor of at least one polynomial}\,\bigr\}.
    \]
    \item Select a subset of the variables to be zeroed, and substitute $0$ for those variables in the polynomials.
    \item Choose one factor for each $p'_j$ together with a lexicographic order, and compute the Gr\"obner basis of the selected factors.
\end{enumerate}
Zeroing appears twice, in steps~1 and~4, because the two are useful at different stages: zeroing before interreduction and factoring (step~1) can simplify the polynomials and expose better factorizations, while zeroing afterwards (step~4) can be chosen with the factorization in hand to make the Gr\"obner basis of the selected factors easier to compute. Either step may be empty.

\begin{claim}
The resulting Gr\"obner basis is a Gr\"obner basis of an ideal whose variety is a subvariety of the original variety.
\end{claim}
\begin{proof}
Let $I = \langle p_1, \ldots, p_m \rangle \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ be the original ideal, write $Z = Z_1 \cup Z_2$ for the union of variables zeroed in steps~1 and~4, and let $V(Z) = \{a \in k^n : a_v = 0 \text{ for all } v \in Z\}$. Denote the Gr\"obner basis produced in step~5 by $G$, and set $J = \langle G \rangle + \langle v : v \in Z \rangle$. It suffices to show $V(J) \subseteq V(I)$.

\emph{Steps~1 and~4 (zeroing).} For any polynomial $q$ and variable set $W$, the substitution $q|_{W=0}$ agrees with $q$ on $V(W)$, so $V(q|_{W=0}) \cap V(W) = V(q) \cap V(W)$. Writing $\tilde p_j = p_j|_{Z_1=0}$ and $p''_j = p'_j|_{Z_2=0}$, applying this generator-wise gives
\[
    V(\tilde p_1, \ldots, \tilde p_m) \cap V(Z_1) = V(I) \cap V(Z_1),
    \qquad
    V(p''_1, \ldots, p''_m) \cap V(Z_2) = V(p'_1, \ldots, p'_m) \cap V(Z_2).
\]

\emph{Step~2 (interreduction).} Each interreduction step replaces a generator $\tilde p_i$ with $\tilde p_i - h \tilde p_j$ for some $h \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$. Since $\tilde p_i = (\tilde p_i - h \tilde p_j) + h \tilde p_j$, the ideal is preserved, so $\langle p'_1, \ldots, p'_m \rangle = \langle \tilde p_1, \ldots, \tilde p_m \rangle$ and $V(p'_1, \ldots, p'_m) = V(\tilde p_1, \ldots, \tilde p_m)$.

\emph{Step~3 (factorization).} This records the irreducible factors and does not change the ideal.

\emph{Step~5 (factor selection).} For each $j$, the chosen factor (which, after the Step~4 zeroing, we denote $f_j = f_{j,i_j}|_{Z_2=0}$) divides $p''_j$, so $V(f_j) \subseteq V(p''_j)$, and intersecting over $j$,
\[
    V(f_1, \ldots, f_m) = \bigcap_j V(f_j) \subseteq \bigcap_j V(p''_j) = V(p''_1, \ldots, p''_m).
\]
Computing a Gr\"obner basis preserves the ideal, so $\langle G \rangle = \langle f_1, \ldots, f_m \rangle$.

\emph{Combining.} Chaining the inclusions,
\[
    V(J) = V(\langle G \rangle) \cap V(Z)
    \subseteq V(p''_1, \ldots, p''_m) \cap V(Z)
    = V(p'_1, \ldots, p'_m) \cap V(Z)
    = V(\tilde p_1, \ldots, \tilde p_m) \cap V(Z)
    = V(I) \cap V(Z)
    \subseteq V(I).
\]
Hence $G$ is a Gr\"obner basis of the ideal $J$, whose variety is a subvariety of $V(I)$.
\end{proof}

It is worth noting that selecting one factor of each preprocessed polynomial does not reduce the dimension of the solution set; only the choice of which variables to zero does. This idea is formalized in the following claim.
\begin{claim}
Selecting one irreducible factor of each preprocessed polynomial does not reduce the dimension of the solution set, provided the factors are chosen jointly so as to realize a maximal-dimension intersection.
\end{claim}
\begin{proof}
Each preprocessed polynomial factors as $p'_j = \prod_i f_{j,i}$, so its zero locus decomposes as $V(p'_j) = \bigcup_i V(f_{j,i})$. Intersecting over $j$ distributes the union:
\[
    V(p'_1, \ldots, p'_m) = \bigcup_{(i_1, \ldots, i_m)} V(f_{1,i_1}) \cap \cdots \cap V(f_{m, i_m}).
\]
The dimension of a finite union is the maximum of the dimensions of its members, so
\[
    \dim V(p'_1, \ldots, p'_m) = \max_{(i_1, \ldots, i_m)} \dim\bigl(V(f_{1,i_1}) \cap \cdots \cap V(f_{m, i_m})\bigr).
\]
A branch realizing this maximum is obtained by jointly selecting factors $f_{1, i_1}, \ldots, f_{m, i_m}$ whose common vanishing locus has maximal dimension.
\end{proof}

\section{CDCL for system of polynomial equations}

Let us say we have a set of factored polynomials $p_1, \ldots, p_m$ over a field $k$, and we want to validate whether there exists a solution to the system $p_1 = \cdots = p_m = 0$ (or any other property that can be inferred from the minimal hitting sets).
We describe a CDCL-style algorithm that can be used to explore the space of possible minimal hitting sets of the polynomials.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{CDCL for Polynomial Systems}
\label{alg:cdcl}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require A set of polynomials $p_1, \ldots, p_m$ over a field $k$.

    \State Preprocess the polynomials
    \State Factor the polynomials into irreducible factors
    \State Initialize $C = \emptyset$ an unsatisfiable cores set
    \State Initialize $S = \emptyset$ a solutions set

    \For{each minimal hitting set $F$ of the polynomials}
        \If{The minimal hitting set $F$ contains an unsatisfiable core in $C$}
            \State \textbf{continue} to the next minimal hitting set
        \EndIf

        \State Check if the minimal hitting set $F$ is satisfiable
        \If{The minimal hitting set $F$ is satisfiable}
            \State Add the solution to the solutions set $S$
        \Else
            \State Extract a minimal unsatisfiable core $U$ from the minimal hitting set $F$
            \State Add $U$ to the unsatisfiable cores set $C$
        \EndIf
    \EndFor

    \Return the solutions set $S$ and the unsatisfiable cores set $C$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A \emph{minimal hitting set} is a set $F$ of factors that hits every polynomial: for each $p_j = \prod_i f_{j,i}$ at least one of its factors $f_{j,i}$ lies in $F$. Committing to $F$ imposes the subsystem $\{f = 0 : f \in F\}$, whose variety $V(F) = \bigcap_{f \in F} V(f)$ lies inside $V(p_1, \ldots, p_m)$ (Claim~1); minimality means no proper subset of $F$ hits every polynomial.

\textbf{Order of iteration over the minimal hitting sets}: the order is from the simplest to the most complex to check for satisfiability. As a heuristic for this cost we use the B\'ezout bound $\prod_{f \in F} \deg f$: it bounds the number of isolated solutions and, for a regular cover, the solving degree of the Gr\"obner computation that decides satisfiability, whose Macaulay matrix grows with that degree. Iterating covers by increasing B\'ezout bound therefore tries the easy covers first.

\subsection{Finding the unsatisfiable cores}
\textbf{unsatisfiable core} is a subset $U \subseteq F$ of factors with no common zero, $V(U) = \bigcap_{f \in U} V(f) = \emptyset$; equivalently $\langle U \rangle = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the unit ideal. We detect this from the reduced Gr\"obner basis of $U$, which equals $\{1\}$ exactly when $1 \in \langle U \rangle$.

Such a core is worth learning because the property is monotone under inclusion: if $V(U) = \emptyset$ then every hitting set $F' \supseteq U$ has $V(F') \subseteq V(U) = \emptyset$ and is therefore also unsatisfiable. Recording $U$ lets the algorithm skip every later hitting set that contains it, the conflict-driven step of Algorithm~\ref{alg:cdcl}.

Given an unsatisfiable $F$, we extract a minimal core by deletion: consider its factors in turn, remove one, and recompute the Gr\"obner basis; keep the smaller set when it remains the unit ideal, otherwise restore the factor. After one pass over the $|F|$ factors every remaining factor is necessary --- dropping any one restores a solution --- so $U$ is a \emph{minimal} (inclusion-irreducible) unsatisfiable core. Minimality here is irreducibility, not minimum cardinality; a smallest core is NP-hard to find and unnecessary for soundness of the pruning.
