\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Motivation: why solving polynomial systems is important, and what we want to find
Systems of multivariate polynomial equations appear whenever a symbolic model contains nonlinear algebraic constraints.
Applications include robotics, coding theory, optimization, statistics, and physics \cite{sturmfels2002solving}.
In many of these problems, an isolated solution is not enough.
One needs the dimension of the solution set, its irreducible components, or the exact relations that hold on every solution.
These are questions about the variety and its defining ideal, rather than about a single point \cite{cox2015ideals,kreuzer2000computational}.

% History of the field and various methods
Elimination by resultants provided an early systematic way to attack polynomial
systems, but the introduction of Gr\"obner bases gave a common algorithmic
framework for elimination, ideal membership, dimension, and related
tasks~\cite{cox2015ideals,kreuzer2000computational}.
Buchberger developed this framework in his 1965 thesis \cite{buchberger2006thesis}.
Later algorithms changed how the expensive reductions are organized.
F4 replaces individual polynomial reductions with sparse linear algebra, while F5 uses signatures to avoid many reductions to zero \cite{faugere1999f4,faugere2002f5}.
Primary-decomposition algorithms use Gr\"obner bases and factorization to separate components \cite{gianni1988primary}.
Factorizing Gr\"obner-basis methods, split on individual polynomial
factorizations and preserve the solution set; earlier methods also interreduce
and prune redundant branches~\cite{graebe1995factorized,singular2025}.
Numerical algebraic geometry takes a different route: homotopy continuation
computes isolated roots numerically, while witness sets represent
positive-dimensional components \cite{sommese2001numerical,sommese2005numerical}.

% Mention recent works in a sentence; emphasize that the field is still active
Recent work has also explored machine-learning methods for polynomial systems.
Peifer et al.\ trained a reinforcement-learning policy for pair selection in
Buchberger's algorithm \cite{peifer2020learning}. Kera et al.\ trained
transformers to compute Gr\"obner bases directly for a restricted class of
zero-dimensional radical ideals \cite{kera2024learning}. Yet these works are
limited to small and specific polynomial ideals.

% What's the gap?
Despite this progress, large systems such as a system that came up in our
research of quantum gravity can remain inaccessible.

% Technical description of the method - hitting sets
Here, we introduce a solver-agnostic framework that breaks down a polynomial
system into smaller branches by factoring and interreducing its equations.
The framework enumerates the minimal hitting sets of the resulting factor sets;
each hitting set contains at least one factor from every equation and therefore
defines one branch of the original solution set. An exact branch solver then
either describes that branch, including any positive-dimensional families, or
certifies that it is UNSAT.
% Needs a better justification then just for Gr\"obner basis computation, besides that the complexity is in the number of variables which our method doesn't directly address
Since the worst-case complexity of computing a Gr\"obner basis is doubly exponential in the number of variables~\cite{mayr1982complexity}, breaking down a large system into smaller branches can be substantially faster than solving the original system directly.

% Theoretical and experimental backing
We prove that refinement terminates and preserves the full
solution set. We compare the resulting implementation with
direct Gr\"obner-basis computation and symbolic decomposition on generated
benchmarks. We also apply the framework to a system from
quantum field theory: with the \texttt{facstd} backend, the framework completes
the computation, whereas standard standalone solvers timed out.
