\begin{abstract}

% [solving large sets of polynomial equations is important for problems in X Y Z]
Polynomial systems are a fundamental language for describing algebraic and geometric problems in mathematics and its applications.
Beyond finding individual solutions, one often seeks global properties of the solution space, such as its dimension and irreducible components, or structural properties and decompositions of the underlying polynomial ideal.
% [many of those problems are still inaccessible \ldots]
For large polynomial systems with many variables and high degrees, determining these global properties can become intractable.
% [leading approaches include X Y Z] and [benchmark relative to other methods]
Leading approaches include variants of Buchberger's algorithm with syzygy, decomposition techniques, resultants, and cylindrical algebraic decomposition.
% [however, these approaches are limited by \ldots]
However, even the most modern approaches still often fail on practical problems or slight perturbations of standard benchmarks.
% [Describe our approach]
Here we introduce a framework that divides a polynomial system into simpler sub problems.
% [broadest strongest claim] and [we demonstrate it on X Y]
The framework only assumes the solver used on each sub problem describes satisfiable branches and certifies unsatisfiable (UNSAT) ones.
If the solver also returns an UNSAT core, the framework can use it to prune later branches, following the same idea as conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL).
In our experiments, we use Gr\"obner-basis solvers and apply the method to a system arising from research in quantum field theory for which no standard method in our comparison succeeds.
% [outlook sentence on what would this contribute to in the figure / extensions for future work]
Together, these ideas offer a path toward the exact analysis of previously inaccessible polynomial systems.

\end{abstract}
