\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{subsec:experimental-setup}

We implemented Algorithm~\ref{alg:Branching} in Rust 1.98.1 and used
Symbolica 3.0.1 for polynomial arithmetic over the
rationals~\cite{ruijl2023symbolica}. Refinement applies the pruning of
Subsection~\ref{subsec:branching-framework}, and the branch solver
processes the terminal
branches fewest generators first. In all experiments, we solve the branches
with a Gr\"obner-basis computation. The available implementations are M4GB,
F5, GVW, F4, and Singular 4.4.1~\cite{makarim2017m4gb,faugere2002f5,
gao2016gvw,faugere1999f4,singular2025}.
A basis containing $1$ certifies that the branch is UNSAT.
The comparison also includes six direct whole-ideal
Gr\"obner-basis configurations and three decomposition methods from Singular
and Macaulay2 1.26.06~\cite{singular2025,macaulay2}.

We ran the benchmark on an Apple M4 Pro with 14 CPU cores and 24 GB of memory.
Every table entry is a single run. Every method received 60 seconds per system.

For factor branching, $T$ is end-to-end wall time and $F$ is the
time to first SAT branch, measured from the start of the
minimal-hitting-set search.  Factorization and input processing are
therefore included in
$T$ but not in $F$.
The complete measurements are in the \texttt{results} directory of the
repository listed under Data availability.

\subsection{Baseline systems}
\label{subsec:baseline-systems}

The baseline has 20 generated systems in six families.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Signed-distance cycles and affine-factor hypergraphs test
    many inexpensive branches.
  \item The overlapping random multiples use seeded dense quadratic
    factors that recur across equations, with no factor shared by
    every equation.
  \item For gated systems, we create a new variable and multiply
    every equation in Cyclic, Katsura, or dense quadratic by
    it~\cite{bjorck1991cyclic,boege1986examples}.
  \item This produces one easy selector branch and one nonlinear
    residual branch.
\end{itemize}

% [Theortical explanation of when factor branching and variable
% zeroing are a good choice]
Factor branching is a good choice when factorization produces branch
ideals that are
substantially easier than the expanded input ideal.  A large number of hitting
sets can still be manageable when individual branches are cheap to solve;
the positive affine family shows that the hitting-set count alone is not
decisive.  The method is less effective when one branch retains the difficulty
of the original nonlinear system, as Cyclic-7 does.

% [Back up the explanation with current or new experiments]
The measurements of time to first SAT branch support this
explanation.  On positive affine
$(12,10,6)$, $F$ is $0.003$ seconds despite the 833 hitting sets.  On
gated Katsura-8, the first SAT branch is found in $0.010$ seconds, but
the full run takes $2.575$ seconds.  Factorization therefore finds the easy
component quickly, while the nonlinear residual controls completion time.

\subsection{Size-graded families}
\label{subsec:scaling-families}

% [Families whose factors come from the problem, grown until every
% method times out]
The baseline families were built to have factor structure.  The following
five families inherit it from the problem instead, and we grow each until every
method times out.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The reduced cyclic $n$-roots systems \texttt{Singular.rcyclic\_$n$}, a
    variant of the cyclic $n$-roots problem~\cite{bjorck1991cyclic}, taken
    unchanged from
    SymbolicData\footnote{\url{https://github.com/symbolicdata/data},
    commit \texttt{a5cdf53}.}.
  \item Nash equilibria of $n$-player games with two pure strategies per
    player~\cite{sturmfels2002solving}.  Each player $i$ either plays a pure
    strategy, $p_i(p_i-1)=0$, or is indifferent, so its payoff difference
    vanishes.  The payoffs are random integers in $[-9,9]$ (generic) or in
    $\{-1,0,1\}$ (degenerate).
  \item Critical points of a random quadratic program $f$ on every face of the
    box $[0,1]^n$, given by $x_i(x_i-1)\,\partial_i f=0$.
  \item Equilibria of a random Lotka--Volterra community on every species
    support~\cite{hofbauer1998evolutionary}, given by
    $x_i\bigl(r_i+\sum_j a_{ij}x_j\bigr)=0$ with $a_{ii}<0$.
\end{itemize}
Each size is a single seeded instance.  The setup is that of
Subsection~\ref{subsec:experimental-setup} with four differences.  Factor
branching entries give $T$ only.  The direct class adds Singular's \texttt{std}
in degrevlex (Singular dp) and omits standalone F4, which remains a factor
branching backend.  A method that times out at one size is not run at larger
sizes.

% [Factor branching loses only on the smallest sizes, and its lead grows with n]
Table~\ref{tab:scaling-classes} shows the same pattern in every family.  The
baselines win only on small systems, each of which they finish in under
$0.5$ seconds.  From rcyclic-10, $n=4$ for the Nash games, $n=7$ for
box-QP, and $n=10$ for Lotka--Volterra, factor branching is fastest, and its
lead grows with $n$: on rcyclic it rises from $2.3$ times at $n=10$ to
$372$ times at $n=18$.  Only factor branching completes rcyclic-19 and
box-QP with $n=8$.  The two families reach this point differently.  Every
rcyclic system refines into only 3 terminal branches, each far easier than
the whole system.  Box-QP refines into up to $3^n$ branches, 6534 at $n=8$,
and each branch is cheap, as in the positive affine family.

% [With a decomposition engine as backend: compare against the same
% engine alone]
Factor branching can also use a decomposition method as its branch solver.
Table~\ref{tab:scaling-decomposition} compares \texttt{facstd} as a backend
with \texttt{facstd} alone.  It also compares each new algorithm with the
Singular command that computes the same object:
Algorithm~\ref{alg:PrimaryBranching} with primdec, and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:Branching} with \texttt{minAssGTZ}.  Both members
of these two pairs run in one Python process that
drives one Singular process for the whole run.  Refinement interreduces in
lex order, and every Gr\"obner basis is in degrevlex.  The implementations use
the optimizations described after each algorithm.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:Branching} with \texttt{minAssGTZ} interreduces
$P$ first, solves the
branches fewest generators first, lets only positive-dimensional primes prune
later branches, and prunes a branch only if it contains another as a set.
Its branch solver $\mathcal B_{\min}$ computes a factorizing Gr\"obner
basis~\cite{graebe1995factorized}, returns each linear piece as a prime, and
decomposes every other piece with \texttt{minAssGTZ}~\cite{gianni1988primary}.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:PrimaryBranching} runs Algorithm~\ref{alg:IdealReduce}
with the factorizing Gr\"obner basis as its branch solver, takes the minimal
primes of the reduced system from Algorithm~\ref{alg:Branching} with
\texttt{minAssGTZ}, and uses the separators and the zero-dimensional shortcut
of Subsection~\ref{subsec:primary-decomposition}.  It runs primdec on
the reduced system
only when the separators detect an embedded component, and marks its
components with the minimal primes.

\texttt{std} does.

\subsection{Physical System}
\label{subsec:physical-system}

The system is an order-five approximation constraining graviton couplings in
asymptotic-safety models to remain consistent with quantum field theory
(QFT)~\cite{rafiezinedine2018simplifying,vacca2010asymptotic}. Solving this
system helps determine admissible fixed-point structures in quantum
gravity~\cite{vacca2010asymptotic}. It has 28 equations in 21 variables, and
its coefficients involve $\pi$. One coupling, $\kappa_h$, must be nonzero.
Factor branching and the Singular and Macaulay2 baselines
impose this by saturating by $\kappa_h$; the standalone M4GB, F5, GVW, and F4
runs, which cannot saturate, add the Rabinowitsch generator
$1-s\kappa_h$~\cite{rabinowitsch1930nullstellensatz}.

For this larger system, every run, including the standalone baselines, has a
120-second total limit.  Within it, factor branching gives its saturation of
the system five seconds and places no further limit on a branch.  Refinement
removes $\kappa_h$ from every factor set and drops a branch once $\kappa_h$
reduces to zero modulo its generators, since neither step loses a point with
$\kappa_h\neq0$.  The branch solver receives each terminal branch with the
generator $1-s\kappa_h$.

% [PrimaryBranching: the primary decomposition of the physical system]
Without a time limit and without the optional separators,
Algorithm~\ref{alg:PrimaryBranching} returns a minimal
primary decomposition of the physical system over $\mathbb Q(\pi)$.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:IdealReduce}, with $\kappa_h$ kept nonzero as in
Subsection~\ref{subsec:sat-reduction}, reduces the 28 equations to 18
generators in three rounds and 0.6 seconds. The saturation of these generators
by $\kappa_h$ has 56 generators.  Algorithm~\ref{alg:Branching} with
\texttt{minAssGTZ} finds the six minimal primes in 0.2 seconds, and
\texttt{primdecSY}~\cite{shimoyama1996localization} decomposes the saturation
in 213 seconds.  On the unreduced system, \texttt{primdecSY} did not finish in
30 minutes when $\kappa_h\neq0$ was encoded by a Rabinowitsch
generator.  Six of the ten components are isolated, in two
families of dimensions 13, 12, and 8.  In one family they are prime.  In the
other, each component arises from its radical by replacing one generator, a
variable, by its square, which doubles the degree.  Each family also has two
embedded components, one of dimension 10 inside its 12-dimensional component
and one of dimension 8 inside its 13-dimensional component.  A Gr\"obner basis
of the saturation confirms that the components intersect to
$\langle P\rangle:\kappa_h^\infty$.  Every step uses lex order with
the variables ordered by a
min-fill heuristic on the supports of the equations.  With the variables in
alphabetical order, Algorithm~\ref{alg:IdealReduce} stops at 27 generators,
because the factorizing Gr\"obner basis of the largest hitting set does not
finish within 120 seconds.
