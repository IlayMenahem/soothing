\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Motivation: why solving polynomial systems is important, and what
% we want to find
Systems of multivariate polynomial equations appear whenever a
symbolic model contains nonlinear algebraic constraints.
Applications include robotics, coding theory, optimization,
statistics, and physics \cite{sturmfels2002solving}.
In many of these problems, an isolated solution is not enough.
One needs the dimension of the solution set, its irreducible
components, or the exact relations that hold on every solution.
These are questions about the ideal $I$ generated by the equations
and its variety $V(I)$, rather than about a single point
\cite{cox2015ideals,kreuzer2000computational}.

% The two output axes and the methods of the paper on them
The outputs of algebraic algorithms vary along two axes
(Figure~\ref{fig:output_axes}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item How far the output breaks down the ideal: it treats $I$ as
    one piece, decomposes it into ideals that need not be prime or
    primary, or decomposes it into its prime or primary components.
  \item How much information the output retains: whether $V(I)$ is
    empty, properties of $V(I)$ such as its dimension, $\sqrt I$, or $I$ itself.
\end{itemize}

% History of the field and various methods
Elimination by resultants provided an early systematic way to attack polynomial
systems, but the introduction of Gr\"obner bases gave a common algorithmic
framework for elimination, ideal membership, dimension, and related
tasks~\cite{cox2015ideals,kreuzer2000computational}.
Buchberger developed this framework in his 1965 thesis
\cite{buchberger2006thesis}.
Later algorithms changed how the expensive reductions are organized.
F4 replaces individual polynomial reductions with sparse linear
algebra, while F5 uses signatures to avoid many reductions to zero
\cite{faugere1999f4,faugere2002f5}.
Primary-decomposition algorithms use Gr\"obner bases and
factorization to separate components \cite{gianni1988primary}.
Factorizing Gr\"obner-basis methods split on individual polynomial
factorizations and preserve the solution set; earlier methods also interreduce
and prune redundant branches~\cite{graebe1995factorized,singular2025}.
Numerical algebraic geometry takes a different route: homotopy continuation
computes isolated roots numerically, while witness sets represent
positive-dimensional components
\cite{sommese2001numerical,sommese2005numerical}.

% Mention recent works in a sentence; emphasize that the field is still active
Recent work has also explored machine-learning methods for polynomial systems.
Peifer et al.\ trained a reinforcement-learning policy for pair selection in
Buchberger's algorithm \cite{peifer2020learning}. Kera et al.\ trained
transformers to compute Gr\"obner bases directly for a restricted class of
zero-dimensional radical ideals \cite{kera2024learning}. Yet these works are
limited to small and specific polynomial ideals.

% What's the gap?
Despite this progress, large systems remain inaccessible, such as a
quantum-gravity system from our research on which every standalone method in
our comparison times out (Subsection~\ref{subsec:physical-system}).

% Technical description of the method - hitting sets
Here, we introduce solver-agnostic methods for several of these
outputs, all built from the same steps. Each method factors the equations and
enumerates the minimal hitting sets of the resulting factor sets. A minimal
hitting set contains at least one factor of every equation, so it defines a
branch, a smaller system whose solutions are solutions of the original one,
and $V(I)$ is the union of their solution sets. Interreducing and
factoring a branch
can split it further, and the methods repeat the split until it changes
nothing. Each method then calls an exact solver on the branches and combines
the results into its output. Unlike factorizing Gr\"obner-basis methods, which
split on one factorization at a time inside a Gr\"obner-basis computation, the
methods split on all equations at once before calling the solver, so any exact
solver can serve.
Since the worst-case complexity of computing a Gr\"obner basis is
doubly exponential in the number of
variables~\cite{mayr1982complexity}, breaking down a large system
into smaller branches can be substantially faster than solving the
original system directly.

% The variants of the framework: same branches, different branch
% solvers and outputs
Figure~\ref{fig:output_axes} places the methods of this paper on the two axes.
The methods differ in the branch solver and in how they combine the branches.
Factor branching asks the solver to describe each branch, including any
positive-dimensional families, or to certify that it is UNSAT, and so returns
a decomposition of $V(I)$ whose pieces may overlap
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). Prime factor
branching asks for the minimal primes of each branch and returns those of
$I$, which give the irreducible components of $V(I)$ over $K$ and their
dimensions
(Theorem~\ref{thm:prime-branching}). The remaining methods keep the exponents
of the factors, and with them $I$ itself. Ideal reduction uses the SAT
branches to replace generators by smaller members of $I$
(Subsection~\ref{subsec:sat-reduction}). Primary factor branching obtains the
isolated primary components of $I$ from its minimal primes and runs a general
primary decomposition only when $I$ has embedded components
(Subsection~\ref{subsec:primary-decomposition}).

% Theoretical and experimental backing
We prove that every method is exact. Refinement terminates and preserves the
solution set, ideal reduction preserves $I$,
and prime and primary factor branching return the
minimal primes and a minimal primary decomposition of $I$. Every numbered
result is also formalized in Lean~4 with Mathlib, in the repository listed
under Data availability. On generated systems with factor structure and on
size-graded families from SymbolicData and from applications, factor
branching with the best of its backends completes 19 of 20 and 27 of 31
systems within 60 seconds, against at most 16 and 20 for the direct
Gr\"obner-basis and decomposition methods. On a system from quantum field
theory, all nine standalone baselines time out, whereas factor branching with
the \texttt{facstd} backend completes in under a second. Primary factor
branching computes the primary decomposition of this system in under five
minutes, while Singular's \texttt{primdecSY} does not finish within 30
minutes on the original equations.
